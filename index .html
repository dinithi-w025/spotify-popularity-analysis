Genre predicts popularity. How a song sounds barely does.

I analyzed 89,700 Spotify tracks across 125 genres to see whether audio features like danceability and energy can predict popularity. Genre was the strongest signal, and a simple linear regression cut prediction error by about 30%, beating a Random Forest.

Sound has almost no link to popularity

Correlation of each audio feature with popularity. Every value sits near zero.

Instrumental tracks tend to be a little less popular. The rest is close to nothing. Features do relate to each other: energy and loudness move together (+0.76), and acoustic songs are calmer (-0.73 with energy).

Genre makes the difference

Top 10 genres by average popularity (values read from the chart, rounded).

Some of this reflects the dataset, not listener taste. 66% of Iranian tracks and 61% of romance tracks have a popularity of 0, so the model uses those genres as a warning sign.

Simple beat complex

Linear regression cut error by about 30% versus the baseline. Cross-validation matched the single split, so the result doesn't depend on one lucky 80/20 cut. A more complex model did not help. A likely reason, which I haven't tested, is that most usable signal is a genre-level average that a linear model captures cleanly, while deeper models overfit noisy audio features.

Excluding zero-popularity tracks raised the Random Forest's R² from 0.19 to 0.31 (MAE 15.24 to 11.79 against its own baseline). Those tracks add noise audio and genre can't explain, and the narrower model answers a different question: how popular a track is, given that it has some listeners. Without the zeros, instrumentalness becomes its most important feature.

Where the model struggles

In the Random Forest's actual-vs-predicted plot, predictions cluster at a few levels (about 10 to 20, about 35, and about 55 to 60), as if the model is mostly guessing a genre-level average. Very popular songs (70+) are under-predicted, and many true zeros are predicted at 10 to 20. Genre gives a rough level; the rest of popularity isn't in this data.

What this analysis can't tell you

  • The data has no artist fame, playlist placement, or release date, which likely drive much of popularity. Even the best model (R² of about 0.33) leaves most of it unexplained.
  • Duplicate tracks were removed, so each song keeps one genre label.
  • Genre averages can be skewed by small genres and how the data was collected.
  • The vibe groups are slices of one continuous cloud, not sharply separated clusters.
